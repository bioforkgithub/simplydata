Explaining "marks" from "hours" and "breakfast" (180 rows). Each effect below holds the other columns fixed:
  intercept: 32.3, the expected "marks" when every number column is 0 and every group column is at its baseline.
  hours: each 1 more "hours" goes with 2.82 more "marks" (95 % CI 2.29 to 3.34); fewer than 1 in 1000 studies would show this much if there were truly none (p < 0.0001).
  breakfast "yes" against "no": "marks" is 3.21 higher (95 % CI 0.561 to 5.86); about 2 of 100 studies would show this much if there were truly none (p = 0.018).
Method: linear model by least squares, as R lm(marks ~ hours + breakfast).
R squared = 0.403 (adjusted 0.397): the model explains about 40 % of the differences in "marks". The model as a whole (F = 59.8, df = 2 and 177): fewer than 1 in 1000 studies would show this much if none of the columns truly mattered (p < 0.0001).
How well it fits: AIC 1292, BIC 1305: smaller is better, but only to compare models of the same rows (compare_models).
Method: AIC and BIC as R AIC(), BIC().

Checking the model of "marks" (180 rows) in four pictures, as R plot(lm(marks ~ hours + breakfast)):
  1. residuals against fitted values: an even band around 0, with no curve, means the form fits;
  2. normal Q-Q: dots on the line mean the residuals follow a bell curve;
  3. scale-location: a flat line means the spread is the same everywhere;
  4. residuals against leverage: rows beyond the dashed lines (Cook's distance 0.5 and 1) pull the model strongly.
Numbers from statsmodels (OLSInfluence or GLMInfluence); the smooth lines are LOWESS, as R panel.smooth. Pictures help judge a model better than tests of the residuals.
